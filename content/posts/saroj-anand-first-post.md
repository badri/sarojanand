# The Temple That Shouldn't Exist

*On lost engineering traditions, impossible tonnages, and the question we're afraid to ask: what if we've forgotten more than we ever learned?*


There's a temple in Maharashtra that defies explanation. Not because it's old (though it is), or because it's beautiful (though it is), but because of what's *missing*.

The Kailasa Temple at Ellora was carved from a single cliff face sometime around 760 CE. When I say "carved," I mean someone took a mountain and removed everything that wasn't a temple. Top to bottom. Basement to roof.

**Here's the problem:** They removed roughly 200,000 tonnes of solid rock to do it.

Let me put that in perspective. A modern rock quarry using dynamite, bulldozers, and hydraulic excavators can remove about 1,000-1,500 tonnes per day under ideal conditions. At that rate, the Kailasa Temple would take *133 days of continuous work*.

Except they didn't have dynamite. Or bulldozers. Or hydraulics.

They had chisels. And hammers. And, if the traditional accounts are right, about *18 years* to finish the job.

That's 30 tonnes of rock per day. Every day. For eighteen years.

With hand tools.

## The Math Doesn't Math

I'm an engineer by training, so I did the calculation the way engineers do: badly, with a lot of assumptions, but enough to know when something smells wrong.

Let's be generous. Let's say they had 500 workers on site at any given time (a massive crew for 8th century India). That's 60 kilograms of rock per worker, per day.

Sixty kilograms. Not carved. Not shaped. Not transported. Just *removed* and discarded somewhere down the cliff.

Every day.

For 6,570 days straight.

"But wait," you say, "maybe they worked in shifts? Maybe there were more workers?"

Sure. Double the workforce to 1,000. Now each worker only has to remove 30 kg per day. Still, that's hauling a small child's weight in shattered basalt down a cliff face, day in and day out, while someone else is carving **three-story pillars** and **life-size elephant sculptures** out of what's left.

And we haven't even talked about the basement levels yet.

## The Sigiriya Problem

You know what else shouldn't exist? Sigiriya, in Sri Lanka.

It's a 200-meter-tall rock fortress built around 480 CE. Not built *on top* of a rock — that would be pedestrian. This one has its palace *carved into* the rock, with water gardens at the base, ramparts halfway up, and a citadel at the summit that required hauling every stone, every brick, every ounce of plaster up sheer cliff walls.

The hydraulic systems alone are engineering marvels. Pressurized water fountains that still work 1,500 years later. No pumps. Just gravity and genius.

And here's the thing: the same culture that built Sigiriya left *no written records* of how they did it. No blueprints. No engineering treatises. No "Sigiriya for Dummies" papyrus.

It's as if someone built the Hoover Dam and forgot to mention it.

## The Uncomfortable Question

Let me float an idea that'll make archaeologists wince: what if there *was* a systematic engineering tradition in ancient India and Sri Lanka, one sophisticated enough to move mountains with pre-industrial tools — and we lost it?

Not "lost" like misplaced your keys. Lost like the Library of Alexandria. Lost like Greek fire. Lost like Roman concrete (which we only figured out again in the 21st century, two thousand years later).

What if the knowledge to build Kailasa and Sigiriya wasn't mystical or alien, but simply *documented in materials that didn't survive*?

Palm leaf manuscripts rot in a century. Oral traditions die with their last speaker. If your entire engineering corpus is written on perishable media, and your civilization takes a few hard knocks (invasions, famines, climate shifts), you can go from "we can carve mountains" to "we forgot how to read" in two generations.

## The Silurian Hypothesis (Or: The Paranoia Lurking Beneath)

Here's where it gets weird.

There's a thought experiment in climate science called the **Silurian Hypothesis**. The name comes from *Doctor Who*, but the question is dead serious:

**If an industrial civilization existed on Earth 50 million years ago, would we know?**

Think about it. Fifty million years is a long time. Continents drift. Mountains rise and erode. Coastlines drown and resurface. What survives?

Not much.

Plastics degrade in centuries. Steel rusts into oxide dust. Concrete crumbles. Cities built on coastlines (where most are) get swallowed by tectonic subduction. Even radioactive waste — the thing we assume will haunt the Earth forever — eventually decays or gets buried under kilometers of sediment.

What's left? Weird carbon isotope ratios in ancient rock layers. Unexplained spikes in rare earth elements. Maybe some synthetic molecules trapped in deep ice cores, if you're lucky.

But a temple carved into a mountain? A palace on a rock? Those could survive.

And if we found them, if we had no context for the people who built them, we'd look at the math and think:

*"This shouldn't be possible."*



## The Canon We Forgot

I don't actually think the Kailasa Temple was built by a lost 50-million-year-old civilization. (Though it would make a hell of a novel.)

But I do think this: **we underestimate how much knowledge humanity has lost**, and we overestimate how much we've always known.

The ancient world had technologies we're still catching up to. Damascus steel. Roman aqueducts. The Antikythera mechanism (a 2,000-year-old analog computer). The Baghdad Battery. Viking sunstones. Chinese earthquake detectors from 132 CE.

And Kailasa. And Sigiriya. And dozens of other structures that make modern engineers scratch their heads and quietly whisper, "How the hell did they do that?"

Maybe the answer isn't aliens or magic or ancient astronauts.

Maybe the answer is: **they knew things, they wrote them down, and the books burned.**

## The Canon We're Building

Here's the hopeful flip side: we live in an age where knowledge is harder to lose.

Every Wikipedia edit. Every arXiv preprint. Every GitHub repo. Every Stack Overflow answer. Every YouTube tutorial on building a forge in your backyard. We're building a distributed, redundant, globally-accessible canon of human knowledge that *doesn't* rely on a single library in Alexandria or a monastery in Timbuktu.

When future archaeologists (or AI historians, or post-human descendants) dig through our digital strata, they won't have to guess how we carved temples.

They'll have the CAD files.

But they might still wonder: *why did you stop?*

Why did the people who built Kailasa not build a hundred more? Why did the engineers who designed Sigiriya's hydraulics not pass that knowledge down in a form that survived?

And why — if we've learned anything from their silence — are we so confident it won't happen to us?



## Coda: The Trace That Survives

If a civilization existed 50 million years ago, what trace would survive?

The Silurian Hypothesis says: probably not cities. Probably not books. Probably not monuments.

But carved mountains? Basalt temples cut into cliffs? Structures so massive and so simple that they outlast everything else?

Those might survive.

And if you were a member of that lost civilization, and you knew your world was ending, and you wanted to leave a message that would outlast continents —

What would you carve into a mountain?
